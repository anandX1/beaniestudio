"Can my kid play horror games?" is one of the most-searched questions from parents on Roblox, and it deserves a straighter answer than it usually gets. Horror is a spectrum - from cartoonish haunted-house fun to genuinely disturbing content - and Roblox contains all of it. This guide is written for parents of 13-year-olds: what to look for, what to check, which kinds of games tend to suit younger teens, and how we handle these questions in our own horror game.

First, the useful reframe: at 13, the question is less *whether* horror and more *which kind*. Teens this age usually want to be startled and thrilled with friends - the social fun of screaming together - not exposed to realistic violence or dread that follows them to bed. The good news: that first kind is well served on Roblox.

## What makes a horror game teen-appropriate

Use these four filters when scanning any game's page:

- **Stylized vs realistic.** Robotic monsters, cartoon ghosts and jump-scare mazes are playground horror - thrilling, harmless. Games advertising realistic gore or psychological horror deserve a closer look first.
- **Player agency.** Games where you run, hide and outsmart the monster feel empowering - you survived *by your play*. Games built around helplessness and scripted dread hit harder and are better saved for older teens.
- **Round structure.** Short rounds with clear endings contain the fear: it ends, everyone laughs, next round. Endless-dread formats give young players no off-ramp.
- **The social layer.** With friends in voice chat, horror becomes comedy as much as terror - shared screaming is bonding, not trauma. Solo late-night sessions hit very differently. Encourage the squad format.

## The settings that matter more than the game choice

For a 13-year-old, these account settings shape the experience more than which game you pick:

1. **Voice chat.** Roblox voice chat requires age verification, and parents can restrict who a child talks to. Check Settings → Privacy before horror night - and know that in-game proximity voice means strangers' voices can be heard in-game (reportable like any chat).
2. **Content maturity labels.** Every Roblox experience carries a maturity label with content descriptors. Horror games range from Mild to Restricted. For 13-year-olds, Mild-to-Moderate labeled horror is the honest zone.
3. **Who they play with.** A private server with school friends is a fundamentally different (and safer) experience than public lobbies with strangers. Many horror games support private servers cheaply or free.
4. **Session time.** Horror before bed is a classic mistake at every age. The 9pm horror session at a sleepover is a core memory; the 11pm one on a school night is why they are tired tomorrow.

## The kinds of games that suit younger teens

**Hide-and-seek horror** is the sweet spot: cartoon-styled hunters, chase-based thrills, huge friend groups, short rounds. It is the playground version of the genre and by far the most popular with younger teens.

**Obby-style horror** (obstacle-course horror) wraps mild scares around platforming skills - the fear never outruns the fun of the jumping.

**Team survival horror with jobs** - games where the group completes tasks while one threat roams - teach cooperation under pressure. The task focus gives anxious players something to do with their hands, which is exactly what a nervous 13-year-old needs.

That last category is ours. STATIC: Salvage vs Hunter is a 5v1 game: five Scrappers salvage scrap from a wrecked facility, one player is the Hunter. A note on our design choices, from one parent to another:

- **No gore.** Getting caught means knocked down, carried, contained - nothing realistic. The scariness comes from tension and chase, not from blood.
- **No radar, no wallhacks, and the Hunter wins by patience** - it is a game of wits, not violence. We rate the fear level at *spooky-fun*: the monster is scary the way a great haunted house is scary.
- **Voice chat is supported but not required** - the game's core mechanics work fully without saying a word, and muted players are never disadvantaged.
- **Rounds are short** and end with escape or rescue stories, not punishment.

## Five questions to ask instead of banning

Parents who flatly ban horror usually lose the negotiation and the trust. Parents who ask questions usually end up in the lobby with their kid. Ask these five: *Which game specifically?* (names beat "some horror game"). *What is its maturity label?* (visible on every Roblox experience page). *Who is in the lobby?* (school friends versus strangers changes everything). *Is voice on, and who can talk to you?* (checkable in two minutes). *When does it end?* (a round count beats a clock - "three more rounds" is a contract kids keep). A 13-year-old who can answer all five is running their own hobby responsibly, which is the actual thing you are raising.

## The honest bottom line

A 13-year-old with verified settings, a squad of friends, a Mild-labeled chase game and a snack within reach is having one of the great childhood gaming experiences. The same kid solo at midnight in a Restricted-label psychological horror game is having the other kind. The difference is not luck - it is the four checks above, applied once.
